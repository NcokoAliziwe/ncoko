PRP370 Agile evidence

Shezz-App is a salon service platform where customers book appointments, upload hairstyle references, make price offers and earn loyalty rewards. I worked on it in an Agile team at CAPACITI as the UI/UX Designer and Product Owner representative. The five sections below each have evidence and my reflection. Click any image to enlarge it.

Team: Sinazo Ntsimbi (Software Developer / Agile Lead), Vumani Kamanga (Frontend Developer) and Aliziwe Ncoko (UI/UX Designer / Product Owner representative).

1. Agile Team Charter

Reflection

Context. The Agile Team Charter was the first artefact our Shezz-App team produced at CAPACITI. It defined our purpose, roles, ways of working, communication channels, decision-making rules and definition of done. I was named UI/UX Designer and Product Owner representative, responsible for user flows, layouts, accessibility and usability checks, and for reviewing completed interfaces.

Application. The charter recorded a dependency that shaped my work: UI/UX designs must be ready before final frontend development. I scheduled my design tasks ahead of the matching build tasks, and I used the definition of done (acceptance criteria met, responsive behaviour checked, Trello updated and the team's acceptance) as the standard for handing a design over.

Learning. I learned that a charter is a working reference rather than a formality. Agreeing in advance on a decision-making process (consensus first, then a majority vote) and an escalation route meant that process questions did not slow us down during the sprints.

Going forward. The charter notes that the Agile Lead and Product Owner roles were proposed and still needed confirmation. In future I would confirm roles at the first meeting and revisit the charter at every retrospective, as the document itself commits us to do.

Evidence

Our charter for the Shezz-App project covers the team purpose and success definition, roles and responsibilities, ways of working (daily stand-up, sprint planning, review/demo and retrospective), working agreements, communication, decision-making, definition of done, core values, risks, review of the charter and a team commitment with initials from each member.

2. User Stories and Tasks

Reflection

Context. Our requirements were written as user stories with acceptance criteria and split into tasks on Trello. My cards carry the initials AN and the label UI Design, and cover the registration page (Sprint 1) and the service catalogue page (Sprint 2).

Application. The registration story served two users, customers and stylists, so I designed two clear registration paths with understandable validation messages and a layout that works on mobile and desktop. For the catalogue, the acceptance criteria required that category, price and service information be easy to find, which guided my layout decisions. Both cards reached 100% completion.

Learning. The "so that" clause of a user story taught me to design for the user's goal rather than for a list of features. Splitting stories into owned tasks also let design and development progress in parallel, with clear accountability for each part.

Going forward. I would write separate stories where customers and stylists have different needs, and I would agree acceptance criteria with the developers at the start so that validation rules are settled before build.

3. Product Backlog

Reflection

Context. The product backlog was the single prioritised source for every sprint. Each card, such as "Customer registration and secure login" and "Stylist profile management", holds a user story, acceptance criteria and a checklist. As Product Owner representative, I added cards to the backlog and helped ensure they described what customers and stylists need.

Application. The order of work followed dependencies. Registration and login came first because every other feature, including booking, making an offer and loyalty rewards, requires a user account. The service catalogue followed, because customers must see services before they can book them.

Learning. I learned that prioritising means asking what depends on what, not only what appears most valuable, and that acceptance criteria must be specific enough to test. "Receive clear feedback for invalid details" can be verified; a vague criterion cannot.

Going forward. I would add a priority label and a rough size estimate to every card, so the order of the backlog is visible on the board and easier to justify to the team.

4. Sprint Backlog

Reflection

Context. Each sprint pulled a small set of items from the backlog into a sprint backlog with an owner and a due date per task. The board covers five sprints: User Registration and Authentication, Product Catalogue, Booking System, Make an offer, and Loyalty Rewards.

Application. The dates on the board reflect the dependency recorded in our charter. In Sprint 4, for example, the booking page UI design is due on 13 October and the related build task on 14 October, so design is finished before development begins. I tracked progress through checklist counts on each card and used the same information in my daily logbook entries.

Learning. Small tasks with a single owner are easier to complete and verify than large shared ones, and a clear sprint goal gives the whole team one outcome to deliver. The board is only useful when it is kept up to date, which was one of our working agreements.

Going forward. I would display the sprint goal and sprint dates at the top of each list so that anyone reading the board can see its purpose and deadline at a glance.

5. Agile Logbook

Reflection

Context. My logbook records my daily stand-ups for Sprint 1, from 21 to 25 September 2026. Each entry answers three questions: what I completed, what I plan to do next, and what is blocking me.

Application. On 21 September I reviewed the Sprint 1 stories and acceptance criteria. On 22 September I completed the registration page UI for customers and stylists. By 25 September I had verified the work against the Trello acceptance criteria, confirmed that the registration and login tasks were complete, and begun planning the Sprint 2 catalogue UI.

Learning. The three-question format kept me focused on finished work and made me state dependencies early. I recorded that validation requirements needed confirmation from the backend developer, and that the catalogue design depended on accurate service details and images, so these risks were visible before they became blockers.

Going forward. I would maintain the logbook across every sprint and add a short retrospective note at the end of each one (what went well, what did not, and what we will change), so that it captures the full cycle described in our charter.
